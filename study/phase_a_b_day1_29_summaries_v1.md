## Phase A, Days 1 to 7: GPU fundamentals, memory hierarchy, vLLM V1

## Day 1: GPU memory hierarchy and the bandwidth gap

T4 inference is gated by where the bytes live, not by raw FLOPS. On-device HBM measured at 217 GB/s for large transfers, about 68 percent of the 320 GB/s spec, while PCIe in either direction tops out near 9 GB/s, making HBM roughly 23x faster than the host link. That ratio is why vLLM V1 dropped GPU-to-CPU KV swap: recomputing from HBM beats round-tripping over PCIe for nearly any prompt length. A separate matmul sweep over batch sizes 1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024 climbed from effectively 0 to 23 TFLOPS, a 35 percent ceiling against the 65 TFLOPS spec. Extended runs at batch=4096 confirmed the T4 was power-limited at the 70W TDP. The big intuition: low TFLOPS at small batch is not memory saturation, it is underutilization, since SMs need parallel work and warps to hide latency. nvidia-smi sm percent is a time-based metric that lights up even when only one warp runs per SM, so it is necessary but not sufficient.

## Day 2: Roofline confirmation: prefill compute-bound, decode memory-bound

Roofline math for a 7B FP16 model on T4 puts the ridge at 203 FLOPs per byte, decode arithmetic intensity at 1 FLOP per byte (deeply memory-bound, capped near 0.5 percent of peak compute), and prefill on a 2048-token prompt at 2048 FLOPs per byte (firmly compute-bound). Nsight Compute on Phi-2 confirmed both predictions empirically. The dominant prefill kernel, turing_fp16_s1688gemm at 256x128, ran at 83.87 percent compute throughput, 24.59 percent DRAM, and 98.8 percent SM active, sitting near tensor core saturation. The decode kernel, cublasGemvParamsEx, hit 96.10 percent DRAM throughput against just 58.47 percent compute, with 47.3 percent of warp stalls waiting on the L1 instruction queue for global memory. Decode launched roughly 53,000 kernels in the captured window because launch count scales with tokens times layers times ops per layer. Attention sat at 27.82 percent compute and 6.50 percent DRAM but 83.40 percent L1/TEX, latency-bound and cache-heavy, not throughput-bound, which is exactly why Flash Attention's tiling matters more than raw bandwidth.

## Day 3: KV cache as the binding constraint and why paging matters

The point of Day 3 was to make concrete that concurrency on a single GPU is gated by KV cache memory, not compute. The calculator computed per-token KV size as 2 times num_kv_heads times head_dim times dtype_size times num_layers, then swept sequence lengths of 512, 1024, 2048, 4096, 8192, 16384 to derive max concurrent requests under a budget of GPU memory minus model weights minus 10 percent overhead. For a 7B-shaped config (32 layers, 32 KV heads, head_dim 128, FP16) that lands near 0.5 MB per token, so an 8K sequence eats 4 GB of KV cache, and an A100-40GB serves only single-digit concurrency at long context. The fragmentation simulation pitted a contiguous allocator against a paged allocator over a 1500 MB pool: contiguous accumulated holes after frees and could not place 250/350/400 MB requests once the largest contiguous region shrank, while the paged allocator (8 MB pages) only cared about total free pages. That mismatch is the exact problem PagedAttention solves, the bridge to Day 6's vLLM internals.

## Day 4: Interconnect topology and the TP-over-PCIe tax

Day 4 quantified the cost of moving bytes between devices. The host-to-device benchmark over float32 transfers at 1, 10, 100, 500, 1000 MB used .to('cuda') and .to('cpu') with cuda.synchronize fences and 50 iterations per size, landing around 9 GB/s on the T4's PCIe link in both directions, three orders of magnitude below NVLink-equipped GPUs at 600 to 900 GB/s. The thought experiment for a 7B model with TP=2 estimated 2 all-reduces per layer times 32 layers, where a hidden_dim=4096 batch=1 FP16 all-reduce is around 16 KB. At PCIe latency that pencils out near 320 to 640 microseconds per decode step, versus roughly 32 microseconds over NVLink. The interview-grade conclusion is that TP over PCIe can make a small model slower than single-GPU, because the per-step communication tax exceeds the compute split. This is the reason T4 is treated as a single-GPU inference card throughout the residency.

## Day 6: vLLM parameter sensitivity and the prefill regression

Three sweeps mapped where each vLLM knob actually binds. The max-num-seqs sweep on TinyLlama (1, 4, 8, 16, 32, with 66 requests at concurrency 33) climbed cleanly from 84.7 to 488.4 tok/s as concurrency rose, with TTFT p50 dropping from 21.6s at max-num-seqs=1 to 0.36s at 32; the first night's flat 84 tok/s result was a thermal artifact that did not reproduce. The gpu-memory-utilization sweep at 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.95 produced near-identical degradation cliffs at concurrency=4 because TinyLlama's short outputs kept the system pegged in prefill, never letting KV memory become the binding constraint. The headline result came from Experiment 3e on Qwen2.5-3B: a fixed-budget prompt-length sweep from 130 to 1794 tokens fit TTFT(ms) = 37.6 + 0.228 x prompt_tokens with R squared of 0.9966, implying a measured prefill throughput of 4,386 tok/s. That makes a 2,000-token prompt occupy the GPU for around 493ms, the wait every concurrent request inherits, the quantitative seed for Phase B's prefill/decode interference work.

## Day 7: vLLM V1 request lifecycle and the unified scheduler

Tracing v0.6.6 source revealed that V1 replaced V0's SequenceGroup with a flat Request class and pushed the GPU-heavy step loop into a separate EngineCore subprocess, communicating with AsyncLLM over ZMQ via msgpack. AsyncLLM.generate is a thin consumer: it tokenizes via InputProcessor, ships an EngineCoreRequest across the process boundary, and yields outputs back through OutputProcessor. EngineCore.step does schedule, execute_model, get_grammar_bitmask (overlapped with the GPU forward pass), future.result, sample_tokens, update_from_output. The biggest architectural shift: V1 removed the SWAPPED state and the swap-to-CPU path entirely. Preemption now means free all KV blocks and prepend the request to the waiting queue, paying full re-prefill cost on resume rather than a CPU memory transfer. The unified schedule loop budgets prompt and decode tokens identically through num_computed_tokens, with chunked prefill built in. The Orca insight made tangible: continuous batching works because finished requests free blocks in step N that a waiting request grabs in step N+1, all within the same KVCacheManager free pool, governed by ref_cnt and the doubly-linked free queue.

## Phase A, Days 8 to 14: vLLM internals, KV cache deep dive, GQA correction

## Day 8: Patching vLLM V1 to make KV blocks observable

The day produced a working instrumentation patch in vLLM V1's KV cache manager and scheduler that emits four event types: BLOCK_ALLOC, BLOCK_ALLOC_FAIL, BLOCK_FREE, and BLOCK_PREEMPT, each tagged with timestamp, request id, and free/total counts. The architectural diagram was hardened to V1 reality: SequenceGroup is gone, BlockSpaceManager is replaced by KVCacheManager plus BlockPool, SchedulingBudget collapses to a `token_budget` integer, and the SWAPPED state is removed entirely because V1 dropped CPU swap in favor of recompute-only preemption. The placement choice mirrors the design: PREEMPT logs from the scheduler's `_preempt_request()` where decision context lives, while ALLOC and FREE log from the cache manager where memory consequences land. Validation on a single TinyLlama request showed a clean round trip: 17 prompt tokens grabbed 2 blocks at prefill, then 3 more during decode of 50 tokens, freed 5 blocks at completion. The free count returned exactly to 30,278 of 30,278, totals reconciling. The null block at index 0 is why total reads as `num_gpu_blocks - 1`.

## Day 9: Watching the system collapse on purpose

Pushing Qwen2.5-3B to `gpu_memory_utilization=0.45` shrank the block pool to 1,034 blocks, then 80 concurrent long-decode requests drove the system into a 78-second thrashing spiral: 89 preemptions, 1,398 ALLOC_FAIL events, 11 timeouts. Free blocks oscillated between 0 and ~51 (one victim's worth) for the duration. Request 9474796f got preempted six times and discarded 2,156 tokens of decode work, more than the 1,900 it actually needed. The real lesson: preemption is memory-neutral but compute-wasteful, since freeing N blocks lets the victim consume at most N on reschedule. The TTFT cliff sweep took three iterations to capture; only `0.45` utilization with 1,203-token hex prompts (75 prefill blocks each) and `max_tokens=200` produced the textbook flat-then-vertical shape. The cliff landed exactly where math predicted, between c=12 and c=14, since 14 x 75 = 1,050 > 1,021 blocks. Max TTFT jumped from 671ms to 8,735ms, a 13x step function. Continuous batching was confirmed in a separate run: 344 scheduler iterations across 19.6 seconds, batch composition changing every ~57ms.

## Day 10: Polishing the Week 2 deliverable into staff-level shape

The annotated architecture diagram came together as a self-contained six-section document covering V1 request lifecycle (api_server to async_llm via ZMQ to core engine to scheduler to kv_cache_manager to gpu_model_runner), the WAITING/RUNNING/FINISHED state machine with no SWAPPED state, the recompute-only preemption cost model (proportional to prompt length, prefix caching ameliorates), the Phase 1 / Phase 2 scheduler decision logic, instrumentation hook points, and the mini-collapse observation. The "what surprised me" section anchored the document in lived experience: having to drop utilization to 0.45 because `--max-num-seqs 32` kept firing before memory exhaustion at higher settings; the binary 13x cliff at c=12 to c=14; the 51-block oscillation as one victim's KV freed and immediately reconsumed; 11 of 80 requests timing out silently with HTTP looking fine. Capacity planning collapses to `floor(total_blocks / blocks_per_request)`, computed upfront from block math rather than discovered through gradual degradation. Afternoon prep on AWQ and GPTQ set up the Week 3 quantization pivot.

## Day 11: Quantization as a capacity lever, not a speed trick

Self-quantizing Qwen2.5-3B to INT8-AWQ via llm-compressor took 8,085 seconds (2.25 hours) for the 36-layer calibration. The pre-quantized GPTQ-Int4 came from the Qwen org. The roofline hypothesis held cleanly: at concurrency=1 decode-heavy, INT8-AWQ ran 1.70x faster than FP16 (62.8 vs 37.0 tok/s) and INT4-GPTQ ran 2.44x (90.1 tok/s), with TPOT tracking weight-size ratios almost exactly. Prefill-heavy gains were much smaller, 1.26x for INT8 and 1.60x for INT4, because weight-only quantization still dequantizes to FP16 before matmul, leaving FLOPs unchanged. The real production lever turned out to be capacity, not speed. Model weights dropped from 5.79 GiB (FP16) to 3.23 (INT8) to 1.95 (INT4), and that freed VRAM rolled directly into KV cache, raising max concurrency at 2,048 tokens from 94x to 131x to 149x. The mental progression locks in: naive says INT8 is faster; mid-tier says it cuts decode bandwidth pressure; staff-level says it frees KV cache, which raises concurrency, which directly lowers $/token.

## Day 12: Closing the loop with quality, capacity, and cost

Perplexity on WikiText-2 came back at 11.66 (FP16), 11.68 (INT8-AWQ, +0.17%), and 12.57 (INT4-GPTQ, +7.8%). HellaSwag acc_norm at 25% subset showed FP16 and INT8 tied at 64.20% while INT4 dropped to 63.36% (-1.31pp). The qualitative eval over 10 prompts was the decisive evidence: INT8 produced 0/10 failures, indistinguishable from FP16, while INT4 produced 5/10 with a clear pattern. INT4 broke hard on creative writing (2/2, severe repetition loops, the poem stanza repeated four times), broke on factual Q&A (1/2, divergent hallucination through Huygens/Romer/Laplace), broke on code (1/2, syntax error in test harness `("{[)", False]`), and broke on long-context comprehension (1/2). Reasoning held at 0/2 because constrained token distributions survive quantization. The cost model on g4dn.xlarge at on-demand $0.526/hr produced $0.552/M tokens for FP16, $0.411 for INT8 (-25.4%), and $0.304 for INT4 (-44.9%). Production recommendation: deploy INT8-AWQ as a drop-in replacement for FP16 with no observable degradation; restrict INT4 to structured reasoning workloads only.

## Day 13: Prefix caching plus the GQA correction that resets all KV math

The morning's hash-based prefix caching experiment took four iterations to debug, all of them cache contamination. Running multiple syslens in one vLLM process leaked blocks across conditions; sequential hit-ratio sweeps reused variant IDs across conditions and silently turned 25% hit into 100% hit. Fix: restart vLLM per syslen and offset variant IDs per condition. Clean results showed prefix caching has zero overhead at 0% hit (validating V1's default-on choice), and a smooth gradient at higher ratios rather than a step function. At syslen=1000 with 100% hit, TTFT p50 dropped from 2,238ms to 124ms, an 18x speedup, and throughput climbed from 76 to 248 tok/s. Speedup grows with syslen up to 1,000 then drops at 2,000 (12x) because uncached suffix tokens still pay O(N) attention against all cached entries. The afternoon delivered the GQA correction that resets the KV math for the rest of the residency: Qwen2.5-3B has `num_kv_heads=2`, not 16, so per-token KV is 2 x 2 x 128 x 2 x 36 = 36 KiB, not the previously assumed 288 KiB. Off by 8x. The capacity ceiling on T4 is therefore far higher than originally thought, and the binding constraint typically shifts to compute and `--max-num-seqs` before KV exhaustion. FP8 KV would theoretically halve per-token to 18 KiB (T4 doesn't support FP8, so this is a modeling exercise).

## Day 14: Speculative decoding as a regime-dependent decision, not a feature

The compatibility check was the first finding: TinyLlama vocab is 32,000 while Qwen2.5-3B is 151,936, and vLLM correctly refuses to start with "Target and draft model should have the same vocabulary size." That shipped the morning to the n-gram fallback, which has no draft model and zero VRAM overhead. Across every experiment, n-gram spec decode was net negative: 0.92x at concurrency=1 best case, degrading to 0.86x at concurrency=8 as continuous batching closed the underutilization gap that spec decode was designed to fill. The K sweep showed no sweet spot, every value (3, 5, 7, 10) ran 7-8% slower than the 37.51 tok/s baseline, with acceptance rate dropping from 18.6% at K=3 to 5.6% at K=10. The task sweep matched expectations: Q&A 56.7% acceptance, code 24.7%, creative 4.2%. But even Q&A's 56.7% wasn't enough; the verification pass over K+1 positions costs more than committing 1.7 tokens via single-step decode on T4. The same-family Qwen2.5-0.5B draft model launched cleanly (matching tokenizer) but was much worse, 15.1 tok/s and 0.40x speedup, because the 6x target-to-draft size ratio leaves draft forward passes as a large fraction of total cost. The draft model also burned 0.94 GiB of weights and 1.66 GiB of KV headroom, nearly halving max concurrency from 44x to 24x. Recommendation: do not deploy. Spec decode wants larger target models (30B+), bigger target-to-draft ratios, and lower-concurrency regimes than this hardware provides.

## Phase A, Days 15 to 20: performance memo, admission control, capstone

## Day 15: Quantization tradeoff memo

This was a writing day, no new experiments, synthesizing four weeks of data from Qwen2.5-3B on T4 into a nine-section staff memo. The headline finding: INT8-AWQ improves decode throughput by 70% over FP16 but prefill by only 26%, because decode is memory-bandwidth-bound while prefill is compute-dominated under these workload parameters and T4 INT8 Tensor Cores stay underutilized. INT8 perplexity moved from 11.66 to 11.68 with zero qualitative failures across 10 prompts. INT4 jumped to 12.57 perplexity with 5 of 10 prompts failing visibly, mostly creative writing repetition loops and divergent hallucination on factual Q&A. The capacity story turned out more important than per-request speedup: INT8 freed 2.56 GiB for KV, raising max concurrent 4K requests from 47 to 66, a 40% gain. Cost dropped from $0.552 to $0.411 per million tokens. Spec decode was net negative everywhere on this hardware, killing it as a recommendation. Monday-morning ship call: INT8-AWQ plus prefix caching, spec decode off.

## Day 16: Admission gateway and the cliff as divergence ratio

This day shifted the cliff definition from a raw KV utilization threshold to the TTFT p99 over p50 divergence ratio crossing 2.0, which makes the boundary observable from black-box latency rather than internal scheduler state. Built a FastAPI gateway in front of vLLM with token-budget admission: budget equals practical KV pool times 0.65, which traces back to Day 9 where divergence began breaking out around 65 to 70% utilization. The big conceptual move was framing this as a memory budget expressed in tokens, not a concurrency cap, since 10 hundred-token requests and 10 eight-thousand-token requests are not the same load. The gateway uses Qwen's own tokenizer with chat template applied, asyncio lock for the budget counter, byte-by-byte SSE proxying without buffering, and a real 429 path with Retry-After. Three smoke tests passed: small request admitted, two concurrent admits, forced-budget rejection with counter unchanged. The interview-critical writeup was the limitations section: gateway estimate is a conservative external proxy, not an engine mirror, blind to prefix cache hits, preemption events, and the prefill versus decode temporal asymmetry.

## Day 17: Reconciliation and Policy B progressive release

The reconciliation experiment compared three numbers side by side across three request shapes: gateway charge versus actual tokens used versus vLLM peak block allocation. The overestimates were dramatic: Shape A 154% over actual tokens and 205% over blocks, Shape B 22% over tokens but 439% over blocks because of prefix cache invisibility, Shape C 43% and 155%. The token budget was reframed as max_prompt_length times max_concurrency rather than just concurrency, which is what makes admission resilient to heterogeneous request sizes. Two distinct overcharge sources separated cleanly: max_completion waste dominated Shape A, prefix cache invisibility dominated Shape B. Built Policy B for progressive release, freeing excess every 50 generated tokens with a SAFETY_MARGIN of 64 to prevent cascading over-admission. In the 8K-budget experiment with 100 short-completion requests, Policy B freed only 77 tokens across 46 admits, basically nothing, because completions finished before the first release checkpoint at token 100. Useful negative result: Policy B is necessary but only pays off under long completions. Also added per-key sliding window rate limits and a deliberately naive FIFO queue as a Day 19 setup.

## Day 18: Load test where admission control made things worse

Day 18 ran identical Locust traffic against the gateway in baseline and controlled modes at 50, 100, and 200 users with a five-bucket two-dimensional matrix mixing prompt length and max_tokens. At 50 users both modes were identical, TTFT p50 around 160ms, no rejections, no preemptions, system far below capacity. At 100 users the controlled mode performed worse than baseline: TTFT p99 hit 5,100ms with 32% rejection rate, while baseline ran at 340 to 570ms with zero rejections. The gateway queued and rejected 32% of traffic that vLLM would have served fine, adding 2 to 5 seconds of queue wait. Root cause: Qwen 3B on T4 has 217K tokens of KV cache, generous enough that the preemption cliff never materialized at 100 users, so the 65% target was over-conservative for this hardware-model ratio. At 200 users vLLM crashed in both modes from process-level overload, not KV exhaustion, exposing that admission control protects one resource and is blind to connection limits and asyncio task explosion. Policy B did fire correctly under min_tokens-forced long completions, freeing 11,672 tokens during the controlled 100u run.

## Day 19: Adversarial testing and the design note

Three experiments fed Portfolio Deliverable 4. Experiment 1 swept arrival orderings with Tenant A sending 5 short requests and Tenant B sending one 12,288-token monster against a 15,211-token budget. All requests admitted in every case, but TTFT diverged 20x by ordering: Tenant A averaged 2,019ms when the large request landed first, 102ms when smalls landed first, 110ms interleaved. Same total demand, same budget, same admission outcome, completely different user experience because the large prefill monopolized GPU compute. The takeaway: the budget controls capacity but not the distribution of capacity, and FIFO is not fairness. Experiment 2 set out to show HOL blocking but accidentally proved a different point: small requests bypassed the queue via the immediate-admission path while the large request sat alone and timed out at 5 seconds. The convoy effect is structurally present in _drain_queue but the experiment did not exercise it. Experiment 3 swept load from 25 to 90% budget utilization. p99 first diverged from p50 at 70% with a 1.5x ratio, then exploded to 20x at 80% (200ms p50, 3,980ms p99), full collapse at 90%. Recommended operating point: 60 to 65%, which is exactly where the original target sat.

## Day 20: Phase A capstone, interview prep, portfolio audit

No new experiments. Polished the four portfolio deliverables and answered all 10 exit questions out loud in mock-interview format, then distilled the Frontier Lab Interview Brief in the 6/3/3/2/2/1 structure. The synthesis crystallized six operating insights worth carrying forward: gpu_memory_utilization applies to total VRAM not post-weights, the Day 9 cliff between concurrency 12 (671ms max TTFT) and 14 (8,735ms) is what admission control exists to prevent, decode versus prefill quantization gains split along the roofline (1.70x decode versus 1.26x prefill for INT8), the gateway overestimates KV usage by 154 to 439% so Policy B is necessary, admission control is blind to scheduler-internal compute contention as proven by the Day 18 100-user result, and arrival order is a 20x policy lever. Caught two important mistakes during the audit: GQA head-count error (used 16 attention heads instead of 2 KV heads, an 8x KV memory overestimate), and incorrect framing that prefill versus decode causes memory contention when the actual blind spot is compute contention. Two V1 mental-model shifts: recompute-only preemption with no swap path, and try-and-fail allocation rather than watermark-based prediction.

## Phase B Track 1, Days 21 to 29: cliff, retry storm, autoscaling

## Day 21: KV cache exhaustion is bimodal

The headline finding from Day 21 was that the same workload exhibits two qualitatively different failure modes depending on the KV budget. At gpu_memory_utilization=0.45, the 16,352-token KV pool ran out at six concurrent 2,258-token requests: KV hit 92.5%, V1 recompute-only preemption fired, and throughput halved from 101 to 51 tok/s while preemption count climbed from 48 to 502 across c=6 to c=32. At gpu_memory_utilization=0.90, the 203,520-token pool never filled past 56% even at 128 concurrent requests, but running requests plateaued at 50 and the queue grew to 76 with zero preemptions, a pure compute saturation. Same prompts, different bottleneck, depending on the ratio of KV memory to per-request prefill cost. The instrumented num_gpu_blocks reading of 12,951 also revealed that vLLM's runtime profiler allocated 7.4% more KV than the Phase A theoretical estimate had predicted, which set up the GQA correction the next day.

## Day 22: Postmortem one and the GQA correction

Postmortem one made the bimodal framing explicit and locked in the math that grounds every later number in Phase B. The memory-bound mechanism is the V1 positive feedback loop: a preempted request loses its KV, re-enters the wait queue, and on rescheduling must recompute the entire prefill, re-consuming the same blocks it just freed. The diagnostic signature is queue depth at zero with preemption count rising. The compute-bound mechanism is the inverse: queue depth rising with zero preemptions, KV utilization stuck around 56%, and per-request prefill saturating GPU cycles. The Why-My-Budget-Was-Wrong section corrected the GQA error: Qwen2.5-3B uses two KV heads, not sixteen, so the correct per-token KV cost is 2 times 2 times 128 times 2 times 36 layers, which is 36,864 bytes or about 36 KiB per token. That fix turned 2,044-token nonsense into the 16,352-token budget that predicted failure at exactly 5.9 concurrent requests, matching observed collapse at six.

## Day 23: Prefill/decode interference is a tail problem

Day 23 quantified how much short requests suffer when long ones share the batch, and isolated chunked prefill as the cheap fix. Isolated short-only TTFT p99 was 230ms. Mixed traffic with chunked prefill disabled drove short p99 to 1,052ms, a 4.57x penalty. Re-enabling chunked prefill (the vLLM 0.17.1 default) dropped that to 446ms, a 2.13x penalty and a 57.6% improvement at exactly zero throughput cost: long-request throughput stayed at 778 tok/s in both configurations. The mechanism came out of the iteration_tokens histogram, not nvsys: average tokens per iteration was 411 in non-chunked mixed versus 276 in chunked mixed versus 32 in short-only. A 2,048-token prefill creates a roughly 504ms decode starvation window for every co-batched short request, derived from the Day 21 fit TTFT equals 37.6 plus 0.228 times prompt tokens. p50 is essentially identical across all conditions; the interference is purely a tail phenomenon for the unlucky 5-10% whose decode step lands inside a long prefill iteration. KV utilization sat at 3-16% throughout, which proves this is a scheduling fairness problem and not a memory pressure problem.

## Day 24: The latency cliff and the divergence ratio

Day 24 mapped the cliff with enough resolution to defend an operating point. Using gpu_memory_utilization=0.60 to constrain the KV pool to 74,416 tokens with 512-token homogeneous prompts, the divergence ratio TTFT p99 over p50 jumped from 1.21 at c=108 to 2.06 at c=113, just 1.7 percentage points of KV change from 85.6% to 87.3%, while p99 leapt from 3,200ms to 7,587ms. That defines the cliff at 87% KV. Throughput peaked at 879 tok/s at c=108 then fell to 786 at c=113 and never recovered. The recommended operating point is 79% KV at c=95, where p99 is 2,115ms with zero preemptions and 803 tok/s, an 8.6% throughput cost for stability. The eight-percentage-point safety margin is dominated by scheduler jitter: at fixed c=113 the KV utilization oscillated plus or minus 15 percentage points around the mean. The recompute-load-over-forward-progress ratio crossed 30% at the cliff and 75% above c=128, which is the regime where the engine is doing more makeup work than new work.

## Day 25: Consolidation and admission control retrofit

Day 25 stitched the three Week 5 findings into one frame and turned them into a defensible admission control design. The unification is memory scheduling under contention: KV exhaustion, prefill/decode interference, and the cliff are three faces of the same coupling between memory allocation, scheduling fairness, and stability under load. The compound failure crystallization is one sentence: interference raises effective concurrency, which accelerates KV exhaustion, which triggers recompute preemption, which re-consumes blocks and further raises concurrency, with no self-correcting term. The retrofit replaces the static Phase A token-budget gate with real-time vllm:gpu_cache_usage_perc as primary signal, hard-rejects with HTTP 429 at 85% KV (cliff minus two), and tiers thresholds by prompt length: short up to 82%, medium up to 77%, long up to 72%. There is also a chunked prefill gate that defers a long request when the short queue exceeds ten. The required Prometheus scrape interval is less than half the TTFT SLO, otherwise the controller reacts to stale data inside the 15pp jitter window.

## Day 26: Retry storm, deduced not measured

Day 26 was deliberately a thinking day rather than a GPU day because the retry storm mechanism is fully deducible from the Day 24 cliff and a geometric series. Amplification factor equals the sum of timeout-rate to the i for i from zero to max-attempts minus one. At a 40% timeout rate with three attempts, that gives 1.56. Effective load equals base load times amplification: a system at 60% capacity with amplification 1.56 sits at 93.6%, past the 87% cliff. Recovery is a drain problem, not a convergence problem, because retries already in flight are indistinguishable from real requests and continue to consume KV blocks even after the trigger ends. The defense stack has four layers, each addressing a distinct part of the problem: client exponential backoff plus jitter to break synchronization, gateway retry budget capping aggregate retries at roughly 10% of successful throughput, admission control hard rejection at 85% KV as a circuit breaker, and the vLLM scheduler as last resort. Production alert: amplification factor above 1.3 sustained for thirty seconds.

## Day 27: Postmortem two and the wrong-signal proof

Postmortem two reframes retry cascade as a second feedback loop that wraps the server-internal preemption loop from the client side. The thesis: the trigger is transient, the cascade is self-sustaining. A 30-second burst at 60% base load drives effective load to roughly 94% via 1.56 amplification, crossing the 87% cliff, and the system stays degraded for two to three minutes after the burst ends because three conditions must clear together: in-flight retries must complete, the poisoned queue must drain, and KV utilization must fall below the cliff. Inference is uniquely vulnerable because retries concentrate into one shared KV pool rather than diluting across a stateless fleet, and request lifetimes are seconds not milliseconds, so the timeout window during which a duplicate can fire is large. Day 27 also nailed the autoscaling signal argument analytically rather than empirically: at the Day 24 cliff with TTFT p99 at 7,587ms, CPU stayed at 20-40% because CPU only handles tokenization and bookkeeping while GPU does the work. CPU-based HPA at 70% would never fire during a KV exhaustion event.

## Day 28: Three timescales and the queue-depth surprise

Day 28 measured the three numbers that govern reactive autoscaling viability and found that queue depth, the supposed leading indicator, did not actually lead. Cold start was 94.2 seconds, dominated by weights load plus CUDA graph capture plus block pool allocation. With max_num_seqs=160 and offered concurrency stepping to 130, queue depth stayed at zero through the entire cliff event while TTFT p99 climbed to 16,573ms, because the scheduler admitted everything to the running set immediately and degradation happened inside via preemption cycling. KV utilization scrapes read 4.0% during a 15,841ms TTFT moment because the metric catches troughs in rapid block-free-and-reconsume oscillation. SIGTERM behavior was the second surprise: vLLM does not close connections, send EOF, or return errors; it just stops sending bytes, and clients hang for the full 30-second timeout before retrying. The composed scale-down hazard math: force-terminating 20 in-flight requests times 1.56 amplification times 1.05% KV per request equals 33 percentage points of pressure on the surviving pool, pushing 79% to 112%, well past the cliff. The Δ-greater-than-t-cold inequality fails on this hardware, so reactive autoscaling is structurally inadequate and pre-warmed standby is required.

## Day 29: Autoscaling memo and mistakes log

Day 29 turned the Days 27-28 measurements into a defensible autoscaling policy. The composite trigger is queue_depth greater than 10 AND kv_util greater than 76% sustained for 30 seconds, with each threshold tied to the cliff arithmetic: Q equals 10 because it is roughly 10% of the safe c=95 operating point, K equals 76% because it is the 87% cliff minus 10 queued requests times 1.05% KV per request, leaving the worst-case admission landing just under the cliff. The policy mandates max_num_seqs at or below 95 so queueing actually forms at the admission boundary, otherwise the queue depth signal is blind. Reactive scaling fails because t_headroom is seconds while t_cold is 94.2 seconds, so a hot standby is required to take t_cold off the critical path. Scale-down uses graceful drain: 503 with Retry-After during the DRAINING state, 3.2-second per-request soft drain target, 120-second hard kill, plus 10-minute scale-down hysteresis. The mistakes log was seeded with the GQA 8x error from Day 13, V0 versus V1 terminology drift, T4 BF16 incompatibility, the cliff-as-divergence-ratio reframe from Day 16, and the GPU-util-as-scaling-signal misconception confirmed on Day 28.
